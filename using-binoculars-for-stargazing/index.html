Using Binoculars for Stargazing

Why binoculars are often recommended as a first stargazing tool over a telescope, based on official NASA skywatching guidance.

Stargazing StartOct 9, 2026 · 3 min read

If you're deciding what to buy before your first real stargazing session, NASA's own skywatching resources make a specific, somewhat counterintuitive recommendation: skip the telescope and start with binoculars instead. Here's the reasoning directly from NASA's guide, and what it means for actually using them under a night sky.

NASA's actual recommendation

NASA's skywatching tools page states it plainly: "Often the answer to the question, 'What is the best telescope for a beginning observer?' is 'A pair of binoculars.'" The reasons NASA gives are practical rather than about image quality: binoculars offer "portability and ease of use, in addition to their generally lower pricetag, compared to telescopes."

What binoculars are actually good at

NASA's guide notes binoculars "will show you a wider area of sky than a telescope, which can be an advantage when observing objects like comets and close pairings of the planets with the Moon." A telescope's narrow field of view can make it difficult to find anything without already knowing precisely where to point, while binoculars show enough surrounding sky that you can locate an object by first finding nearby reference points.

Specific sights NASA lists as visible through binoculars: "star clusters, double stars, and the four largest moons of Jupiter, along with stars too faint to see with your eyes alone." Spotting Jupiter's four largest moons (often called the Galilean moons) with ordinary binoculars is a frequently cited "first win" for new stargazers, since it's a visibly different and more detailed sight than anything achievable with the naked eye, but still achievable without a telescope.

The control that matters most: focus

NASA's guide makes a direct comparison here: "As the main control mechanism is simply to focus them, binoculars tend to be easier to use than all but the simplest telescopes." A telescope often involves separate controls for focus, magnification, and tracking an object's apparent movement across the sky as the Earth rotates. Binoculars have essentially one control to learn.

Why mounting them changes the experience

NASA's documentation adds a specific, practical note: "Binoculars can be handheld, but they are a much more useful tool when stably mounted on a tripod. (They tend to be more enjoyable to use when mounted, as well!)" Handheld binoculars amplify the natural shake of your hands along with the magnified image, which becomes noticeable and tiring during extended observation. A simple tripod mount removes this shake entirely and, according to NASA's own phrasing, makes the experience "more enjoyable," not just more stable.

An even simpler alternative NASA mentions

For people who find even binoculars' two eyepieces inconvenient to adjust, NASA's guide mentions a specific alternative: "An even easier option is a spotting scope, or monocular... they remove the need to adjust the two eyepieces to get a clear view." Spotting scopes and monoculars are more commonly associated with birding and hunting, but NASA's documentation notes they work for skywatching too, trading some of binoculars' depth perception for a single, simpler eyepiece to adjust.

A practical first binocular session

  1. Start with the Moon. It's bright, easy to find, and the detail increase over naked-eye viewing is immediately obvious, which is a good way to confirm your binoculars are properly focused before trying something fainter.
  2. Try for Jupiter's four largest moons, using NASA's own list of what's achievable, once you've confirmed Jupiter is visible that night using a monthly skywatching guide.
  3. Mount on a tripod if you have one, even an inexpensive photography tripod, since NASA's documentation specifically links stability to enjoyment, not just image clarity.
  4. Scan for star clusters or double stars in a dark-sky area once you're comfortable with focus and framing, since these were specifically named as targets suited to binocular viewing rather than requiring a telescope.

Key takeaways

  • NASA's own skywatching guidance recommends binoculars over a telescope for beginners, citing portability, ease of use, and lower cost.
  • Binoculars show a wider field of view than most telescopes, which makes locating objects easier, especially comets and planet-Moon pairings.
  • Jupiter's four largest moons, star clusters, and double stars are specifically listed by NASA as achievable binocular targets.
  • Binoculars have essentially one control to learn (focus), compared to a telescope's multiple controls.
  • Mounting binoculars on a tripod removes hand shake and is specifically noted by NASA as making the experience more enjoyable, not just more stable.
